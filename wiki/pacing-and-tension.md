# Pacing and tension

> Rhythm over time: build-up, peaks, rest and a finale. Read before deciding the order of scenes, when threats come, or where scares go.

Players can't stay at full intensity. A good game breathes: tension builds, peaks, eases into rest, then builds again, rising toward a finale. Rest beats are not dead time; they are what makes the next peak land. The same shape works at every scale: a single encounter, a level, the whole game.

## Principles

- **Peaks need valleys (Left 4 Dead's AI Director).** Valve's Michael Booth describes a system that tracks each player's "emotional intensity." It rises when they take damage or fight off enemies nearby, and fades over time. The Director cycles through four states. *Build up* sends threats until intensity peaks. *Sustain peak* holds it for a few seconds. *Peak fade* eases off. *Relax* gives 30 to 45 seconds of calm, then the build-up begins again.
- **Pace the rhythm, not just the difficulty.** The Director changes *how often* threats come, not how strong they are. Rhythm alone makes the game feel alive.
- **Structured unpredictability.** Designers set the rules and the density; the system decides exactly where and when things happen at runtime. Big events are shuffled and never repeat back to back. Players can't learn the script, so tension stays real.
- **Draw the interest curve (Jesse Schell).** Sketch how interested the player is over time. The shape that works: an early hook, then a rising line of peaks with rests between them, then a grand finale. The curve is fractal: the same shape inside each scene, each level and the whole game. Compare the curve you meant with what playtesters actually feel.
- **Rest beats do work.** Calm moments let players recover, take in the world, talk to each other and plan. They make the next threat scarier by contrast.

## For our designs

- Lay out the scenes on the board in play order and mark each as build-up, peak or rest.
- Open with a hook in the first minute, and save the biggest moment for the end.
- After every peak, give a rest beat: a safe room, a reveal, a moment to regroup or laugh together.
- For scares, build anticipation before the payoff, then release with relief. Keep scares light for younger players. (This page's sources cover pacing in general; they are not horror-specific research.)
- Vary when threats come so players can't learn the pattern.

## Common mistakes

- Constant intensity, which numbs players.
- Long stretches of nothing with no anticipation.
- Putting the best moment in the middle, so the ending sags.
- The same threat at the same moment every time.
- No time to breathe after a big moment.

## Questions to ask of a design

- What does the interest curve look like, scene by scene?
- Where is the hook, where are the peaks, and where is the finale?
- What is the rest beat after each peak, and what do players do in it?
- Is there anything predictable enough that players stop feeling it?
- Does each scene have its own small build-up and release?
- Where would a playtester get tired or bored?
