# Belonging

> A home in the game: a place that is yours, people who feel like family, a world you think about during the day and look forward to returning to. Read before deciding what keeps players coming back.

In early World of Warcraft, players felt at home. They had a guild that felt like family, places where they were known, and a world they thought about during the day. That feeling, more than any single mechanic, kept people coming back for years. Research on online games explains why: a good game world can work like the café or pub where everybody knows your name.

## Principles

- **Games can be a "third place" (Oldenburg; Steinkuehler and Williams).** Sociologist Ray Oldenburg described *third places*: informal gathering spots outside home and work, like cafés and pubs. They are open and easy to return to, everyone is equal there, conversation is the main activity, there are regulars, and the mood is playful. Steinkuehler and Williams found online games meet most of these. Teenagers mentor adults, regulars set the tone, and players meet people unlike themselves.
- **An empty world is a lonely bar.** In the same research, deserted towns in Asheron's Call 2 gave players far fewer social benefits. Belonging needs other people to be visibly around.
- **When play turns into work, the home goes.** When guild life became large, hierarchical raiding, it felt like a job and less like a third place.
- **Guilds range from tree house to barracks (Williams and others).** A study of World of Warcraft guilds found small guilds were tight-knit, and about three quarters were founded on real-life friends or family. Large guilds were formal and goal-driven, with more drama and turnover. About a third of guildmates were real-life friends. Players used the game both to keep up real relationships and to make new ones.
- **Virtual bonds become real bonds (Koster).** Raph Koster's Laws of Online World Design hold that social bonds formed in a fictional world grow into real ones. Communities work best up to about 250 people; beyond that, smaller groups form. Owning something in the world gives players a reason not to leave, because social bonds alone are not always enough.
- **Belonging can outlive the game (Pearce).** When the online world of Uru closed, its players called themselves refugees, moved to another world together and rebuilt their culture there. People belong to the group, not just the software.

## Thinking about it during the day

Belonging reaches outside play time. Gordon Calleja (see Immersion) counts thinking about and planning for a game as part of being involved in it. Players who have something waiting for them, such as a friend, a plan, a place they built or a story to continue, come back because they want to, not because a timer tells them to.

## For our designs

- Give players a place that is theirs: a base, a room, a corner of the world that remembers them.
- Give them people: a group, a team, a recurring cast who know their name.
- Make it easy to come back and pick up where they left off.
- Leave something to look forward to: a plan with friends, a project half-built, a mystery still open.
- Keep the world feeling lived in, with others visible even in a small game.
- In a single-session or family game, the "home" might be the group itself. Give the family rituals, nicknames and in-jokes the game supports.

## Common mistakes

- Pulling players back with pressure (timers, fear of missing out) instead of desire.
- A world that feels empty, or forgets the player between sessions.
- Turning group play into a chore with obligations and schedules.
- Hierarchies that make some players feel they don't matter.

## Questions to ask of a design

- What does a player have in this game that is theirs?
- Who would notice if the player didn't come back?
- What would a player think about during the day, away from the game?
- What is waiting for them when they return?
- Does the world feel lived in?
- Is the group's time together play, or work?
