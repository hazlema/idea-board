# Social presence

> Other people being there: playing together, against each other, side by side or just being seen. Often overlooked, often what players remember most. Read before deciding how players share the game.

Many players come for the people, not the game. Social presence is the feeling that others are there with you, whether you are working together, competing, watching each other or simply sharing a world. Research finds that people in groups feel more, and feel it more strongly, than people playing alone. Even players who play alone want an audience.

## Principles

- **People Fun is a reason to play in its own right (Lazzaro).** Nicole Lazzaro's study of players found four keys to emotion in games, and one of them is the people. Players use games to be with others: rivalry and wisecracks, teamwork and camaraderie, the pride of watching a friend or child succeed. Players said "it's the people that are addictive, not the game" and "I want an excuse to invite my friends over." The biggest surprise: groups showed more emotion, and more intense emotion, than solo players.
- **Being seen matters, even without teamwork ("Alone Together").** A study of World of Warcraft found that many players spent much of their time playing alone, yet among others. Having an audience, feeling others around you, and spectacle made solo play feel social. Showing off what you achieved to people who understand it is a strong draw.
- **Players want different things, and those wants mix (Bartle, Yee).** Richard Bartle described four kinds of players: achievers, explorers, socialisers, and killers (who act on other players). Too many killers drive socialisers away. Nick Yee's survey of about 3,000 players found three broad motivations (achievement, social, immersion) that combine instead of excluding each other. A player can love both teamwork and winning.
- **Design the social situation, not just the rules (Isbister).** Katherine Isbister argues games move us through coordinated action, role-play and social situations, online and in the same room. The "isolated gamer" is a stereotype: much play happens with others.
- **Shared, equal presence can create warmth (Journey).** Jenova Chen said that the more people he met in World of Warcraft, the lonelier he felt, because gear and loot set players against each other. In Journey, your companion is anonymous and everyone is equal. You talk only in chirps, which also recharge your partner's scarf, and staying close warms the visuals and adds instruments to the music. Players later posted thanks to strangers they had shared it with.
- **Cooperation can be made tangible.** In Journey, being near each other literally helps (the chirp recharges the scarf). The more a game makes helping each other *do* something, the more it feels like togetherness.

## For our designs

- Decide who plays together, and where: same couch, same room, online or taking turns.
- Give every player a reason to need the others: shared goals, different roles, abilities that combine.
- Make it fun to watch. Spectators, younger siblings and waiting players should enjoy what they see.
- Create moments to show off and celebrate each other: a reveal, a shared win, a scoreboard of funny moments.
- For families, let strong and weak players both contribute, and let parents enjoy a child's success.
- Watch for mechanics that set players against each other in ways that sour the mood.

## Common mistakes

- Treating multiplayer as single player with more people in it.
- One player doing everything while the others watch.
- Competition that humiliates instead of teasing.
- Communication tools that let players be cruel.
- Forgetting the people in the room who aren't holding a controller.

## Questions to ask of a design

- Who is this game played with, and where?
- What does each player need from the others?
- When do players laugh together, cheer each other or tease each other?
- Is it fun to watch, not just to play?
- Can someone show what they achieved to people who care?
- Could a quieter or less skilled player still feel part of it?
- Does anything here push players apart?
