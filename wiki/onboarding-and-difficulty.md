# Onboarding and difficulty

> Teaching by doing, keeping challenge matched to skill, and failing without frustration. Read before deciding how the game starts, teaches or gets harder.

Players should learn the game by playing it, not by reading about it. After that, the job is to keep the challenge in step with their growing skill: too hard breeds anxiety, too easy breeds boredom. In between is flow, where players lose track of time. Different players have different sweet spots, so good games let players tune the challenge without feeling judged.

## Principles

- **Flow is the channel between anxiety and boredom (Csikszentmihalyi).** Total, energised focus needs clear goals, immediate feedback and a challenge that matches the player's skill. If the challenge rises above skill, the player gets anxious. If skill rises above the challenge, they get bored.
- **Every player's flow zone is different (Jenova Chen).** One fixed difficulty curve serves only the average player. Chen's fix is to build the choice into play itself, so players set their own challenge as they go, without stopping for menus. In flOw, players choose when to dive deeper into harder layers.
- **Teach in small skill steps, each with a use (Daniel Cook).** A *skill atom* is one small loop: decide, act, see the result, understand a little more. Atoms chain: one learned skill unlocks the next. If a skill is learned but has no interesting use, players stop caring (burnout). Burnout early in the chain blocks everything after it.
- **Introduce, develop, twist, conclude (Nintendo).** Koichi Hayashida describes Mario level design as *kishōtenketsu*. Introduce one idea safely, develop it, add a twist, then wrap it up before it goes stale. Each level carries one clear idea all the way through. Mark Brown traces the same four steps through Super Mario 3D World.
- **Let the first moments teach (Miyamoto).** World 1-1 of Super Mario Bros. was built so players "gradually and naturally understand what they're doing," and then start to play freely. "Once the player realizes what they need to do, it becomes their game."
- **Offer help without judgement (Celeste).** Celeste's Assist Mode lets any player slow the game, add stamina or extra air dashes, or become invincible. It changes the rules around the player instead of cutting content. It was renamed from "Cheat Mode" because that name felt judgmental.
- **Remove barriers before the fun.** Chen notes that something like a fiddly 3D camera can drive casual players away before they ever reach the good part.

## For our designs

- Make the first minute playable with no instructions, and teach the first idea by doing it.
- Introduce one new idea at a time, give it a use, then build on it.
- Let players choose their own level of challenge inside play (a harder path, a bonus risk), not only in a menu.
- Make failure quick, clear and cheap: show why it happened and let the player try again at once.
- For mixed groups (kids and adults), give each player a way to contribute at their own level.
- If there is a help or easy option, word it kindly.

## Common mistakes

- Walls of instructions before any play.
- Teaching several new ideas at once.
- Teaching a skill that is never used again.
- Difficulty spikes with no warning.
- One difficulty for everyone, especially in family games.
- Failure that costs too much time, or doesn't say what went wrong.

## Questions to ask of a design

- What does a new player do in the first 30 seconds, and what does it teach them?
- What is each new idea, where is it introduced, and where is it used?
- Where might a player get anxious, and where bored?
- How can a stronger and a weaker player both enjoy the same session?
- When a player fails, do they know why, and how fast can they try again?
- What stands between a new player and the fun part?
