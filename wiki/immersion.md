# Immersion

> Being absorbed in the game: its senses, its challenge or its world. One part of a good game, not the whole of it. Read before deciding on atmosphere or what might break it.

Immersion is how deeply the player is drawn in. Research shows it comes in levels, each with barriers, and in different kinds: a game can absorb you through its sights and sounds, through its challenge, or through its story and characters. It matters, but it is not the same thing as a good game. Some of the best-loved games score low on it.

## Principles

- **Three levels: engagement, engrossment, total immersion (Brown and Cairns).** Each has barriers. *Engagement* needs access (controls and feedback the player can master, a genre they like) and the player's investment of time and attention. *Engrossment* needs a well-built game (interesting tasks, visuals, plot) that moves the player's emotions. *Total immersion*, "you feel like you're there," needs empathy with the character and an atmosphere where graphics, sound and plot fit what the character is doing. Total immersion is fleeting.
- **Three kinds: sensory, challenge, imaginative (Ermi and Mäyrä, the SCI model).** *Sensory*: the audio and visuals overpower the real world (Half-Life 2 scored highest). *Challenge-based*: a satisfying balance between challenge and ability (Nethack and Civilization III scored highest). *Imaginative*: absorption in the story, world and characters (Knights of the Old Republic II scored highest). Most games mix the three. Choose which ones our game leans on.
- **Immersion is not the measure of a good game.** In the same study The Sims 2 scored lowest on immersion, and the authors warn against calling Half-Life 2 the better game for it: casual and social games work differently. Presence, social play and belonging often matter more.
- **"Immersion" is too vague to design with (Calleja).** Gordon Calleja breaks involvement into six dimensions: control (kinesthetic), finding your way (spatial), other people (shared), story (narrative), emotion (affective) and goals and decisions (ludic). It covers play away from the game too: planning and thinking about it. Name the dimension you are designing for instead of saying "immersive."
- **Barriers come in order.** If the controls are frustrating, no story can immerse the player. Fix access first.

## For our designs

- Pick the kinds of immersion the game leans on (sensory, challenge, imaginative) and support them deliberately.
- Keep atmosphere consistent: sound, colour, words and rules should all belong to the same place.
- Remove friction early in the ladder: clear controls and feedback come before atmosphere.
- Give the player a character they can care about or be.

## Common mistakes

- Calling a design "immersive" without saying which kind, or how.
- Spending effort on atmosphere while the controls still frustrate.
- Breaking the mood with things that don't belong: modern UI jargon in a spooky world, a joke at the scariest moment.
- Assuming more immersion always means a better game.

## Questions to ask of a design

- Which kind of immersion does this game lean on: sensory, challenge or imaginative?
- What could stop a new player at the first barrier (controls, feedback, genre)?
- Does every screen, sound and line of text belong to the same world?
- Who is the player meant to empathise with?
- What would break the mood, and have we removed it?
- If immersion were low, what else would make this game worth playing?
