# Core loops and choices

> The loop players repeat, the decisions that make it interesting, and why players come back. Read before adding or changing a mechanic.

A game is what players *do*, over and over. That repeated cycle is the core loop: act, see what happens, learn, act again. Inside it, the game lives or dies on its decisions. A decision is interesting when it has a real tradeoff and the answer depends on the situation. Players keep coming back while the loop still teaches them something.

## Principles

- **Design from the feeling backwards (MDA).** Hunicke, LeBlanc and Zubek split a game into *mechanics* (the rules), *dynamics* (how those rules behave once players act on them) and *aesthetics* (what players feel). The player meets the feeling first; the designer builds the rules. Start from the feeling you want, then find rules that produce it. Instead of "fun," name the kind: sensation, fantasy, narrative, challenge, fellowship, discovery, expression or submission.
- **Watch for runaway leaders.** MDA's example is Monopoly. The player in front keeps pulling ahead, so the game is decided long before it ends. Rules that let trailing players catch up keep everyone playing.
- **A game is a series of interesting decisions (Sid Meier).** A choice is not interesting if everyone always picks the same option, or if it may as well be random. Good decisions trade one thing against another: risk against reward, now against later. In Civilization, a Wonder is slow to build but lasts forever; a chariot is quick but matters less.
- **Give players what they need to choose, and answer every choice.** Meier says to err toward too much information rather than too little, and to acknowledge every decision, even with just a sound. Choices should let players show a style, cautious or bold. Mix heavy and light decisions so players are neither swamped nor bored.
- **Loops last; arcs are used up (Daniel Cook).** A *loop* repeats and keeps paying off through mastery (chess). An *arc* is experienced once: a cutscene, a story reveal, a scripted moment. A game built mostly of arcs needs a constant supply of new content. Most story games sandwich them: arc, loop, arc. Levels act as arcs that give the core loop fresh variations.
- **Fun is learning, and mastery ends it (Koster).** Raph Koster argues the fun comes from the brain learning patterns. Once a pattern is fully mastered, the game turns boring, as tic-tac-toe does. A loop stays fresh while it still has something to teach. Ian Bogost and others argue "fun" is too narrow a goal; it is one lens among several.

## For our designs

- State the core loop in one line: "search a grave, find a clue, choose a path."
- Name the feeling each mechanic exists to create.
- For every decision on the board, write the tradeoff. If there isn't one, it isn't a decision.
- Ask what keeps the loop interesting on the tenth playthrough. That might be new combinations, other players, or harder variations.
- Keep the game to a few core mechanics that interact, not many that don't.

## Common mistakes

- "Choices" with an obvious best answer.
- Decisions made without the information needed to make them.
- Mechanics added because they sound cool, not because they serve the feeling.
- A game built of one-off moments (arcs) with a thin loop under them.
- The leader running away with the game.

## Questions to ask of a design

- What is the core loop, in one sentence?
- What feeling is each mechanic for?
- What does the player give up with each choice?
- Would two different players choose differently here? Why?
- Does the player know enough to choose well?
- What is still left to learn after an hour? After ten?
- Can a player who falls behind still get back in?
