# Presence

> The player feels they are really there and that what they do matters. Read before deciding how the world responds to the player.

Presence is the feeling of *being somewhere* through a game, to the point where the player stops noticing the screen and controller. Researchers split it into two parts: feeling that you are in the place, and feeling that what happens there is really happening, and happening to you. A world can look perfect and still lose presence if it ignores the player.

## Principles

- **Presence is "the perceptual illusion of nonmediation."** The player forgets the medium. Lombard and Ditton also count *social* presence here: people react to on-screen characters as if they were real, even knowing they are not. Players grieve for virtual pets like a Tamagotchi.
- **Two separate illusions: place and plausibility (Slater).** *Place illusion* comes from the world answering the body correctly: look, move or reach, and the view and sound respond as they should. *Plausibility* comes from events that relate to you, meaning the world notices and reacts to you, and from a scenario that matches what the player expects could happen. When both hold, people behave as if it were real.
- **The world must react to the player.** Of the two, plausibility is the one game designers most often lose. Characters who look at you, doors that remember you opened them, a town that talks about what you did: these build plausibility. A beautiful world where nothing responds to you feels like a painting.
- **Agency is seeing that your choice mattered (Murray).** Janet Murray calls agency the satisfying power to take meaningful action and see the results of your decisions. Pressing buttons is not agency. The player has to *see* the consequence, and see that it came from their choice.
- **Presence is fragile and comes in moments.** It is strongest at peak moments, not as a constant state. Design for the moments (an arrival, a reveal, a close call) instead of expecting it all the time.

## For our designs

- Give the player a body or a role in the world, even a simple one (a costume, a name, a reason to be there).
- Make the world acknowledge the player: reactions to where they go, what they pick up and what they decided.
- Make consequences visible and traceable to a choice: "the ghost is gone *because* you lit the lantern."
- Keep the rules of the world believable on their own terms. A spooky graveyard can have ghosts, but they must behave consistently.

## Common mistakes

- Treating presence as graphics. Good looks give place illusion; it is reactivity that sells plausibility.
- Choices whose results are invisible or come so late the player can't connect them.
- Characters that ignore the player, or say the same line whatever just happened.
- Breaking the world's own logic for convenience (an invisible wall where a fence should be).

## Questions to ask of a design

- Where does the world notice the player, and react?
- Can the player point to something that is different because of what they chose?
- Who is the player in this world, and why are they here?
- What would make a player forget they are holding a controller or phone, even for a moment?
- Does anything here break the world's own rules?
- Which moments are meant to make the player feel "I'm really here"?
