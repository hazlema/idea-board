# Shared stories

> The moments players retell afterwards: stories that come out of play, rituals and inside jokes. Read before deciding how story and events happen in the game.

The stories players tell each other after playing are often worth more than the story the game tells them. "Remember when the ghost grabbed Dad?" Those stories come from play itself: systems that collide, characters that react, surprises nobody scripted. A game designed for retellable moments spreads by word of mouth and builds the bonds described on the Belonging page.

## Principles

- **Players want their story, not a story (Koster).** Rickey's Law in Raph Koster's collection says players don't want "a story," they want *their* story. An authored plot is known to everyone within days. Systems that give players room to make their own stories keep giving.
- **Designers are narrative architects (Jenkins).** Henry Jenkins describes four ways games carry story. *Evocative* spaces draw on stories players already know (a haunted graveyard). *Enacted* stories unfold as players act. *Embedded* stories are placed in the world for players to find. *Emergent* stories come out of play. In The Sims, characters' conflicting desires create drama from simple household objects.
- **Build story generators, not only stories (Sylvester).** Tynan Sylvester frames RimWorld as a story generator rather than a game, inspired by Dwarf Fortress. Players read more meaning into events than the simulation actually holds. That is a strength: leave room for imagination, and simple pieces make rich stories.
- **Narrative Legos (Levine).** Ken Levine proposes breaking story into small pieces that recombine. Characters have clear passions that shift with what the player does, and their goals conflict, so helping one hurts another. An orc who hates elves likes you more or less depending on how you treat elves.
- **Shared experience creates shared stories (Journey).** Journey players posted thanks and apologies to strangers they had travelled with. A moment lived through together becomes a story both people tell.
- **Groups create rituals (Lazzaro).** Players in groups invent their own rituals and behaviours around a game. Leave room for them: celebrations, nicknames, house rules.

## For our designs

- Design for at least one "you had to be there" moment per session.
- Let systems collide in ways that surprise even the designers.
- Give characters clear wants that react to what players do.
- Make dramatic reversals possible: a near miss, a last-second rescue, a betrayal, a lucky break.
- Help players remember and share: a recap of the night's funniest moments, a photo, a name for what happened.
- Leave gaps for imagination instead of explaining everything.

## Common mistakes

- Every playthrough goes the same way, so there is nothing new to tell.
- Story only in cutscenes, separate from what players did.
- Systems so predictable that nothing surprising happens.
- Explaining everything, so there is nothing to imagine.

## Questions to ask of a design

- What will players tell their friends about afterwards?
- Which moments came from what players did, not from a script?
- What two systems could collide to surprise players?
- What does each character want, and how does that change with the player's actions?
- How could two sessions of this game turn out differently?
- What helps a group remember and retell what happened?
